Comparative history · from Hammurabi to the present

Slavery, a history shared by every civilization

Mesopotamia, Rome, China, Korea, the caliphates, African kingdoms, the Ottoman Empire, the Crimean Tatars, the Barbary corsairs, the Aztecs, the European powers: nearly every major society has practiced slavery. This site sets these systems side by side, with their numbers, their rules and how they ended, so that anyone can compare them on the evidence.

Modern slavery: a figure of a different kind

The ILO, Walk Free and the IOM estimate that 50 million people were living in modern slavery in 2021 (28 M in forced labor, 22 M in forced marriage). This is a stock measured on a given day: it cannot be compared with the 12.5 million of the transatlantic slave trade, which is a cumulative total over 375 years.

The Barbary corsairs and European slaves

For three centuries, corsairs from Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli and Salé captured Christian sailors, fishermen and villagers at sea and along the coasts of Europe, who were then sold or held for ransom in the Maghreb.

An economy of privateering and ransom

The Barbary regencies, more or less autonomous vassals of the Ottoman Empire, made privateering a state enterprise. Captives were put to work in the galleys, on public works or in households, and many were sold back to their families or to religious orders. The Trinitarians (founded in 1198) and the Mercedarians (traditionally 1218) raised funds throughout Christendom to ransom captives.

Algiers alone held more than 30,000 European slaves around 1650. The raids struck Italy, Spain and Provence, but also Ireland, England and Iceland.

The end came from outside. In 1816, Lord Exmouth's Anglo-Dutch fleet bombarded Algiers and secured the release of about 1,200 slaves (the figure varies by source). The French conquest of Algiers in 1830 put an end to privateering.

Robert C. Davis's estimate

In Christian Slaves, Muslim Masters (2003), Davis estimates that "almost certainly a million and quite possibly as many as a million and a quarter" Europeans were enslaved between 1530 and 1780. He starts from observed numbers and assumes that a quarter had to be replaced every year (deaths, ransoms, conversions), or about 8,500 captures a year.

He also argues that between 1500 and 1650, more European Christians were probably taken to Barbary than Africans to the Americas.

The critics

Peter Earle considers that extrapolating from the most intense period (1580-1680) inflates the total. John Wright stresses the fragility of the retrospective calculation. Ehud Toledano faults the absence of Ottoman sources, and Jarbel Rodriguez the conflation of ransomable captives with slaves.

Davis himself acknowledges that his method is the only one possible with the available archives, not the best one.

Key takeaway for comparison

Europeans, too, were the victims of large-scale slave trades, here and in the Tatar raids (2 to 5 million captives). Davis's figure is a high order of magnitude, not a count. Over their full duration, the two trades differ by an order of magnitude: 1 to 1.25 M for Barbary, 12.5 M for the Atlantic. Barbary captives could be ransomed, and their status did not pass to their children.

The transatlantic slave trade and colonial slavery

From 1501 to 1875, European and American ships carried about 12.5 million Africans to the Americas, most of them purchased from African states and merchants. They were enslaved on the plantations of Brazil, the Caribbean and the southern United States.

France: Nantes, Saint-Domingue and the Code noir

France fitted out 4,118 slaving voyages, 44% of them from Nantes, ahead of Le Havre, La Rochelle, Saint-Malo and Honfleur. The Code noir of 1685 provided the legal framework for slavery in its colonies and defined the slave there as movable property.

In 1789, Saint-Domingue had more than 450,000 slaves for about 30,000 whites and 30,000 free people of color, and dominated world sugar production. According to Hilliard d'Auberteuil, a writer of the period, more than 800,000 Africans had been brought there between 1680 and 1776, and only 290,000 were still alive.

After independence, the ordinance of 1825 imposed on Haiti an indemnity of 150 million francs for the benefit of the former colonists, reduced to 90 million in 1838. Together with the associated loans, Haiti kept paying it off until 1921-1922.

A striking demographic contrast

Only about 388,000 Africans landed directly in North America, yet the United States had nearly 4 million slaves in 1860: the enslaved population there grew by about 25% per decade. In Brazil and the Caribbean, it shrank by about 20% per decade (Michael Tadman). The sugar regime could sustain itself only through a continuous influx of new captives.

What historians consider distinctive about this system

A unique combination

No single criterion sets the transatlantic slave trade apart: other trades lasted longer or were of comparable volume. It is the combination that makes it distinctive. Slave status was hereditary and assigned by skin color. The export plantation was integrated into European capitalism. The flow was concentrated over about 350 years, and the descendants of the enslaved and of the enslavers are fellow citizens today.

Slavery and capitalism

Eric Williams (1944) argued that the profits of slavery helped finance the Industrial Revolution. Stanley Engerman puts them at less than 5% of the British economy, and Seymour Drescher shows that the system was not in decline when Britain abolished it. Joseph Inikori, by contrast, argues for the weight of Atlantic commerce as a whole.

The abolitions

France abolished slavery on February 4, 1794; Bonaparte reinstated the slave trade in 1802, and final abolition came in 1848. Britain banned the slave trade in 1807 and slavery in 1833, paying £20 million to the owners; the enslaved received nothing.

From 1808 to 1867, the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron captured about 1,600 slave ships and freed about 150,000 Africans, at the cost of nearly 1,600 sailors' lives. Chaim Kaufmann and Robert Pape see it as "by far the most costly international moral action in modern history."

The Oriental slave trades

For nearly twelve centuries, African captives were carried across the Sahara, through the Red Sea and from the Swahili coast to North Africa, Arabia, the Gulf, Persia and India. It is the longest of the great slave trades, and the most poorly measured.

Why such a gap

For the centuries from 650 to 1400, the figures "can only be guessed," as the historian Klas Rönnbäck points out: the gap stems mainly from assumptions about the Middle Ages, for which there is almost no data. The transatlantic trade, by contrast, has been reconstructed ship by ship. The figures in the "tens or hundreds of millions" that circulate are considered grossly inflated by specialists.

A domestic, military and more female trade

The transatlantic trade deported about two men for every woman. The Oriental trades sought more women, for concubinage and domestic labor. Men served as servants, soldiers or laborers. A minority of boys were castrated to become eunuchs; the mortality of the operation is described as high, but no reliable percentage has been established.

The Muslim world also turned former slaves into an elite: the Mamluks, converted slave soldiers, ruled Egypt from 1250 to 1517.

Plantations remained rare, with two exceptions. The Zanj Rebellion (869-883, near Basra) mobilized more than 50,000 slaves from the salt marshes. In the 19th century, Zanzibar's clove plantations relied on slaves who made up as much as two-thirds of the population.

Why so few descendants?

There are few visible African diasporas in the Middle East. Historians explain this by the castration of some of the men, the high proportion of women and the integration of concubines' children, who were often free. The relative weight of these factors has not been quantified. This contrast with the Americas also partly explains why this trade weighs less heavily in collective memory.

The debate over the "veiled genocide"

Tidiane N'Diaye

In Le Génocide voilé (2008), the French-Senegalese anthropologist puts the number deported to the East at 17 million and speaks of genocide. He contrasts about 1 million descendants with 70 million for the transatlantic trade. His orders of magnitude are close to the literature, but these last two figures are unsourced.

The critics

In a 2010 review, Moritz Behrendt criticizes him for relying almost exclusively on European explorers, for treating very different systems as a single bloc, and for calling the trade "Arab-Muslim" without calling the Atlantic trade "European-Christian." N'Diaye's claim that three-quarters of those castrated died is not supported by any academic source we have found.

Late abolitions

In the Ottoman Empire, enslaved women were still being sold in 1908. Formal abolitions then followed one another: Iran and Jordan 1929, Bahrain 1937, Kuwait 1949, Qatar 1952, Saudi Arabia and Yemen 1962, Oman 1970, Mauritania 1981. In 1948, the Anti-Slavery Society estimated the number of slaves in the Arabian Peninsula at about 1 million. Almost all of these abolitions were obtained under British or international pressure.

Mesopotamia and ancient Egypt

The oldest known law codes already regulate slavery. In Mesopotamia as in Egypt, people became slaves through war, through debt, or by selling themselves to survive destitution.

The Code of Hammurabi

The Code distinguishes three categories of people: the awīlum (fully free man), the muškēnum, and the wardum or amtum, the male or female slave. The slave is property sold with guarantees: laws 278 to 281 govern warranties of sale, laws 15 to 20 punish harboring fugitives, and law 282 provides that a slave who denies his master shall have his ear cut off.

One often-cited rule, law 117, supposedly freed a person sold for debt after three years. It remains to be verified in a scholarly translation. No estimate of the share of slaves in the Mesopotamian population exists.

Egypt: servants more than slaves

The Egyptian terms translated as "slave" refer rather to workers or servants. Slaves could own property and enjoyed certain protections. The New Kingdom saw a rise in the enslavement of foreign captives from Nubia, Libya and Asia.

Slavery was not the dominant mode of production in Egypt.

The pyramids were not built by slaves

Egyptologists agree: the great pyramids were built by conscripted or paid laborers. Herodotus's figure of "100,000 slaves" is unsupported.

Greece: Athens and the helots of Sparta

Athenian democracy rested in part on the labor of slaves, most of them non-Greeks. Sparta dominated a population of helots far larger than its own citizen body.

Athens

The census of Demetrius of Phalerum (317-307 BCE) counts 21,000 citizens, 10,000 metics and 400,000 slaves, a figure most historians doubt. Modern estimates range from about 20,000 to 80,000 or more. The silver mines of Laurion are thought to have employed about 30,000 slaves.

Slaves came from war, piracy and trade, and were mostly "barbarians": on one list of 32 confiscated slaves, 13 were Thracian. In the 6th century, Solon banned the debt slavery of Athenians. Aristotle theorized the slave as a "living tool" and formulated the doctrine of slavery "by nature."

The helots of Sparta

Collectively owned by the state and bound to the land, the helots of Laconia and Messenia handed over a share of their harvests. According to Aristotle, the ephors declared war on them every year, so that the krypteia could kill them without religious pollution. Thucydides reports the disappearance of about 2,000 helots who had been promised their freedom.

Messenia was liberated by Epaminondas in 369 BCE.

Slaves or serfs?

The ancients themselves were uncertain: Critias called the helots "slaves to the utmost degree," Pollux placed them "between the free and the slaves." The historian Jean Ducat speaks of serfdom.

A population sustained by war and birth

According to Walter Scheidel, building up Italy's slave population required 2 to 4 million imported slaves over the last two centuries BCE. About three-quarters of Italian slaves, however, are thought to have been born into slavery.

Legally, the slave was a thing (res), without legal personality, whose testimony was admissible only under torture. The revolt of Spartacus (73-71 BCE) is its most famous episode.

Rome resembles Brazil, not the United States

The ratio of imports to the enslaved population (about 3 to 1) puts Rome closer to slaveholding Brazil than to the southern United States, where the enslaved population grew from 1.2 million in 1810 to nearly 4 million in 1860 with only 600,000 to 650,000 imports (Scheidel).

A real way out

Formal manumission generally conferred Roman citizenship, and the children of freedmen were born free. It was so common that Augustus had to cap it with the lex Fufia Caninia (2 BCE) and the lex Aelia Sentia (4 CE). This is a major contrast with the racial slavery of the Americas.

China: a minority but millennia-old servitude

Slavery runs through the whole of Chinese history, from captives sacrificed under the Shang to girls sold as domestic servants in the 20th century, without ever becoming the foundation of the economy.

From the Han to the Qing

Under the Han, slaves made up only "a very small part of the population," and ownership was capped: 200 for kings and dukes, 30 for officials. The usurper Wang Mang abolished slavery in 9 CE, then restored it in 12.

Tang law forbade enslaving free people, but allowed it for criminals, foreigners and orphans, classed among the "base people" who were legally equated with livestock. At the Manchu conquest, the Qing counted about 2 million slaves. The Yongzheng Emperor freed most hereditary servile groups between 1723 and 1730.

A late and incomplete abolition

Slavery was officially abolished in 1909 (1910 according to some sources), but the practice persisted until 1949, notably with the mui tsai, girls sold as domestic servants.

A hereditary status

Nobi status was passed on by birth and was fed by debt and by self-sale in times of famine. A Seoul register from 1663 counts 73% nobi among registered residents, but it is a local sample.

State nobi were freed in 1801. Hereditary status was abolished in 1886-1887, and the system disappeared in 1894 with the Gabo Reform.

Slaves or serfs?

The numerical criterion

With 30 to 40% of the population, Joseon Korea ranks among the great slave societies, on a par with the southern United States or Rome.

Rights and integration

Nobi could hold property and land, and those who lived outside the master's home had their own households. Kim speaks of "serfdom developed under slavery," James Palais considers them closer to serfs, and Rhee and Yang consider the word "slave" inappropriate.

India: sultanates, agrarian servitude and British abolition

On the subcontinent, slavery took the form of war captives under the sultanates, of exports to Central Asia, and of a hereditary agrarian servitude that fell on the lower castes.

Figures from the chronicles

The chronicles of the Delhi Sultanate put forward figures that are probably inflated: 50,000 young slaves under Alauddin Khalji, 180,000 under Firuz Shah Tughluq. The historian Scott Levi links the export of Hindus to Central Asia to the building of the Sultanate and Mughal states. In the 17th century, the Dutch East India Company also exported 150 to 400 slaves a year from Arakan and Bengal.

Why estimates vary eightfold

For the 1840s, estimates range from about 1.1 million to 8-9 million (Bartle Frere, 1841). It all depends on the definition: should the bonded agricultural laborers of the lower castes, such as the Pulayar or the Paraiyar, be counted as slaves?

Abolition "by denial"

British India withdrew legal recognition of slavery in 1843, then in practice criminalized it through the Penal Code of 1861. The historian Indrani Chatterjee speaks of "abolition by denial," because agrarian servitude largely survived.

Siam and the archipelago: debt and raids

In Siam, slavery was mainly debt bondage, from which people could buy their way out. In the Philippine archipelago, the fleets of Sulu raided the coasts of all Southeast Asia.

Siam: a redeemable servitude

Around 1854, Bishop Pallegoix estimated that a quarter to a third of the roughly 6 million inhabitants were debt slaves. This is an observer's estimate, not a census.

Masters could neither kill nor maim their slaves, who could own property, inherit, enter into contracts, go to court and buy their freedom. The historian Cruikshank considers the Western model of slavery ill-suited to this system.

Chulalongkorn's gradual abolition

King Chulalongkorn carried out abolition in stages. The decree of 1874 freed children born into slavery after 1868 at age 21. The abolition act dates from 1905, the Penal Code of 1908 criminalized the sale of persons, and slavery legally ended in 1915.

The Sulu raids

The Iranun and Banguingui fleets of the sultanates of Sulu and Maguindanao are thought to have enslaved 200,000 to 300,000 people between 1770 and 1870, raiding as far as the Strait of Malacca, Java and southern China. The Malay word lanun, "pirate," comes from the name of the Iranun.

Khiva and Bukhara, slave markets

Until the Russian conquest, the khanates of Khiva and Bukhara were major slave markets, supplied by Turkmen and Kazakh raids against Persians and Russian settlers.

Religion as a boundary

Since Islam forbade the enslavement of Muslims, the Sunni khanates treated Shiite Persians as lawful targets, as they did Christians. The missionary Joseph Wolff counted 200,000 Persian slaves in Bukhara in the 1840s.

Fragile figures

The figure of about 1 million enslaved Persians and Wolff's figure rest on travelers' accounts. They should be taken as orders of magnitude.

The end

Russian slaves were freed in 1840 under pressure from St. Petersburg. The Russian conquest of Khiva in 1873 triggered a slave revolt and the abolition demanded by General Kaufman.

African slave societies

Slavery existed in Africa before the slave trades and even intensified there in the 19th century. States such as the Sokoto Caliphate or the Kingdom of Dahomey were among the great slave societies of their time.

The Sokoto Caliphate

Founded in the early 19th century in what is now northern Nigeria, the caliphate had at least 1 million and perhaps 2.5 million slaves around 1900. Jan Hogendorn and Paul Lovejoy put their share at between a quarter and a half of a population of about 10 million. Sokoto thus ranks among the great modern slave societies, behind the United States of 1860 (about 4 million) and Brazil.

The British administrator Lugard abolished the legal status of slavery in 1901, without compensation.

Dahomey, a player in the transatlantic trade

The slave trade provided most of Dahomey's royal revenue. King Ghezo signed a treaty ending exports only after the British blockade of 1851-1852, and exports resumed in 1857-1858. At court, a genuine debate pitted supporters of the trade against advocates of a shift to palm oil. The ports of Ouidah, Bonny and Luanda together shipped more than 2 million captives across the Atlantic.

Do not generalize

The share of "a quarter to a half" slaves applies to the great slave states of the 19th century, not to all of Africa.

Ethiopia and Mauritania: the last abolitions

Ethiopia was admitted to the League of Nations in 1923 on condition that it suppress slavery. The Italian laws of 1935-1936 freed about 420,000 slaves, and Haile Selassie abolished slavery on August 26, 1942.

In Mauritania, the French abolition of 1905 was not enforced. A 1981 decree abolished slavery without criminal penalties, and a 2007 law made it a crime. Current estimates diverge: 90,000 people according to the 2018 Global Slavery Index, 600,000 according to the BBC in 2017.

Medieval Europe, the Vikings and the Mediterranean

The word "slave" comes from "Slav": in the Middle Ages, captives from Eastern Europe were sold to the Islamic world and Byzantium. Slavery then declined in the West, but not in the Mediterranean.

Vikings and Slavs

From the 9th to the 11th century, the Vikings captured Irish, Anglo-Saxons and Franks, and transported Slavic captives along the Volga and the Dnieper to the Abbasid Caliphate and Byzantium. Dublin became a major market. Oxford's "Dirhams for Slaves" project estimates that slaves were among the main exports of Northern Europe.

In the 10th century, Slavs were the group most present in the trade, hence the Latin sclavus, then the French esclave and the English "slave" (William Phillips).

From decline in the West to the Mediterranean trade

In Western Europe, slavery receded between the 10th and 12th centuries in favor of serfdom: the slave trade was banned in England in 1102, and Sweden abolished slavery in 1334.

The Mediterranean kept slaves into the early modern period. Venice and Genoa dominated the Black Sea slave trade from the 13th century onward, with Caffa in Crimea as the main market. At least 10,000 slaves were sold in Venice between 1414 and 1423. In 1565, Seville had 6,327 slaves for 85,538 inhabitants, most of them African.

The link to the Atlantic

The first Portuguese raid in sub-Saharan Africa dates from 1441. Iberian slavery forms the link between the medieval Mediterranean and the transatlantic slave trade.

The Ottoman Empire: soldiers, harem and household service

In the Ottoman Empire, slavery was primarily political and domestic: elite soldiers, harem concubines, eunuchs and servants. At its height, a fifth of the population of Constantinople was enslaved.

Slaves at the summit of the state

The kapıkulu, slaves of the Porte, including the Janissaries recruited through the devshirme, a "blood tax" levied on Christian boys from the Balkans and Anatolia, numbered about 100,000 in 1609. Slaves could become viziers.

The harem and the eunuchs

Harem concubines were purchased, most often of Christian origin: Circassians, Abkhazians, Georgians. Black eunuchs came from sub-Saharan Africa, white eunuchs from the Balkans or the Caucasus. Prices varied by origin: a young white girl for domestic service cost about 3,000 kuruş, an African girl 1,500 to 2,500 (Madeline Zilfi).

A largely nominal abolition

A firman of 1830 on white slaves, a "cosmetic" closure of the Istanbul market in 1847, a ban on the Circassian and Georgian trade in 1854-1855, then on the trade in Black Africans in 1857, not enforced in Tripoli before the 1890s. Enslaved women were still being sold in 1908.

The Crimean Tatar raids

For more than three centuries, Tatar and Nogai horsemen raided Poland-Lithuania, Ukraine and Muscovy to supply the slave markets of Crimea and the Ottoman Empire. It was one of the largest slave trades of the early modern period, and one of the least known in the West.

A drain on Eastern Europe

According to the study by Volha Charnysh and Ranjit Lall (American Political Science Review, 2026), at least 3.64 million people were captured between 1441 and 1774, and about 5 million if the missing data are estimated. That would represent more than a quarter of the population of Eastern Europe in 1400.

Orest Subtelny notes that some raids carried off as many as 30,000 people, but that the average was closer to 3,000. In Caffa, under Ottoman control from 1475, nearly 20,000 slaves were sold every year in the 1570s (Halil İnalcık).

A new estimate, still open to discussion

The Charnysh-Lall study is the most recent and the highest; it has not yet been debated by specialists. The historian Alan Fisher considers that no reliable total can be calculated, as the sources inflate the numbers.

Russia: from kholops to serfs

Russia knew slavery proper, kholopstvo, followed by mass serfdom that affected more than a third of the population in 1857. Russian serfs were freed in 1861, four years before American slaves.

The kholops, Russians who sold themselves

According to Richard Hellie, kholops made up about 10% of the Muscovite population. He sees it as "one of the most peculiar slave systems in the world," because the slaves were mostly Russians who had sold themselves. Agricultural kholops were merged into serfdom in 1679, household kholops in 1723.

Serfdom

Codified in 1649, serfdom bound peasants to the land and gave landowners near-total rights. Serfs could be transferred without the land, but kept protections over their property and their religion, and access to the courts. Alexander II abolished serfdom in 1861; former serfs often paid more than the market price for their land, and the redemption payments were not abolished until 1907.

1861 and 1865: two abolitions, two systems

The comparison between Russian serfdom and American slavery, pursued by Peter Kolchin, is illuminating but debated. Serfs, who shared the nationality and religion of their masters, were neither legally chattel nor assigned their status by race.

The Americas before and after Columbus

Indigenous American societies had varied forms of servitude. Colonization then enslaved millions of Native Americans, and some Native nations themselves owned Black slaves.

Aztecs and Incas

Among the Aztecs, one became a tlacotli through punishment, war, debt or self-sale, for example during the famine of the 1450s. The slave could marry, own property and even own slaves, and his or her children were born free (Ross Hassig). But captives were sacrificed, and an "incorrigible" slave resold three times could be destined for sacrifice.

Among the Incas, the yanakuna formed a hereditary servitude, sometimes of high status; the word "slave" is a debated translation.

The Northwest Coast

On the Pacific coast of North America, slavery was embedded in hunter-fisher societies and in the potlatch. Leland Donald shows that this servitude fits a cross-cultural definition of slavery; in some groups, up to a quarter of the population was enslaved.

The "other slavery"

Andrés Reséndez (The Other Slavery, Bancroft Prize 2017) estimates that 2.5 to 5 million Native Americans were enslaved over four centuries. His broad definition encompasses the encomienda, the repartimiento, debt peonage and the forced labor that survived legal bans. Alan Gallay estimates that 24,000 to 51,000 Native Americans were exported from Carolina's ports between 1670 and 1715.

Finally, the "Five Tribes" (Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Seminole) owned 4,500 to 5,000 Black slaves in Indian Territory around 1839.

Two findings to keep side by side

A movement born in the West

The United Kingdom devoted considerable resources to fighting the slave trade: from 1808 to 1867, the West Africa Squadron captured about 1,600 ships and freed about 150,000 Africans. Kaufmann and Pape see it as "by far the most costly international moral action in modern history."

Paid to the masters, not to the victims

The British and French abolitions compensated the owners, never the enslaved. In Haiti, it was the former slaves who paid an indemnity to the colonists, until 1921-1922. The fight against the slave trade also served to justify the colonization of Africa.

Why Western memory focuses on the transatlantic slave trade

If slavery was universal, why does public debate in France, the United Kingdom or the United States focus almost exclusively on the transatlantic slave trade? Historians put forward several reasons, and disagree on the value of comparison.

Four reasons

The descendants are citizens. The descendants of Atlantic slaves live in the societies that enslaved them or that emerged from them (the Antilles, the United States, Brazil, Haiti), and press claims for remembrance and reparations there: CARICOM's ten-point plan (2014), the African Union's 2025 year of reparations. The Oriental slave trades left few visible diasporas.

States legislate on their own crimes. France's Taubira law of May 21, 2001 recognizes as a crime against humanity the transatlantic slave trade, the Indian Ocean slave trade and slavery "perpetrated from the fifteenth century onward"; it names neither the trans-Saharan trades nor the intra-African trades.

It is the best-documented slave trade. It is the only one reconstructed voyage by voyage in a public database.

The self-critical tradition of democracies is often invoked, but no specific study establishes it: it is a hypothesis.

Comparing or singling out: the strongest arguments

For a global history

Olivier Pétré-Grenouilleau (Les Traites négrières, 2004) puts forward about 11 million deported across the Atlantic, 17 million for the Oriental slave trades and 14 million for the intra-African trades. Slavery is a near-universal institution, African states were active participants in it, and a history centered on Europe paradoxically remains Eurocentric. He rejects any "Richter scale of suffering" and holds that the slave trades were not genocides, because they aimed to exploit, not to exterminate.

For Atlantic specificity

The Atlantic system was qualitatively different: the slave as chattel, worth 30 to 60% of a plantation's value; a hereditary status assigned by color; natural decline of 20% per decade in the Caribbean; all of this at the heart of societies that proclaimed liberty and equality. Its effects persist: racism, debts such as Haiti's, inequality.

The Pétré-Grenouilleau affair

In 2005, the Collectif des Antillais-Guyanais-Réunionnais sued the historian under the Taubira law, then withdrew its complaint in early 2006. The complaint concerned his rejection of the word "genocide," not his comparative figures. Françoise Vergès, then a member of the Committee for the Memory of Slavery, called the lawsuit "unacceptable." The "Liberté pour l'histoire" (Freedom for History) appeal, signed by nearly 600 teachers and researchers, then called on lawmakers to stop passing judgment on history.

Common ground

Orlando Patterson (Slavery and Social Death, 1982) compares 66 societies and defines slavery without making race a criterion. His work serves both camps: the institution is universal, but racialization is added in the Atlantic. Specialists on Korea, Siam or Sparta point out, moreover, that many systems do not fit the definition of the slave as property, which puts purely numerical comparisons into perspective.

The sociologist Jean-Michel Chaumont, author of La Concurrence des victimes (1997), warns against the race for uniqueness among competing memories.

This site's editorial line

No verdict on which slave trade was "worse" than the others. Each system is described criterion by criterion, with its attributed figures, its debates and its actors on all sides: European, Arab and African merchants, Tatar and Ottoman states, but also revolts, internal debates and abolitionist movements.

Modern slavery · 2021

Slavery today

Banned everywhere, slavery has not disappeared. According to the International Labour Organization, 50 million people were living in "modern slavery" in 2021.

Where?

The 2023 Global Slavery Index ranks North Korea, Eritrea and Mauritania highest for the share of the population affected. The Arab States have the highest rate of forced marriage. G20 countries import about $468 billion worth of goods at risk of forced labor each year, $170 billion of it by the United States.

Why this figure cannot be compared with the slave trades

The 50 million is a number measured on a given day. The 12.5 million of the Atlantic trade or the 8 to 17 million of the Oriental slave trades are cumulative totals over three to twelve centuries. Writing that there are "four times more slaves today than during the entire transatlantic slave trade" is therefore false. Moreover, "modern slavery" includes forced marriage and situations without legal ownership, whereas historical slavery was most often a legal status that could be inherited and sold.

Memory and public debate

Why do we mostly talk about the transatlantic slave trade?

Slavery existed on every continent, and other slave trades lasted as long or were as massive. Yet in France, the United Kingdom or the United States, public debate almost always focuses on the transatlantic slave trade. This is no accident: six reasons, unequally demonstrated, explain it.

1. The descendants live in the countries concerned

This is the reason most often put forward. The descendants of Africans deported to the Americas are citizens of the countries that enslaved them or of those that emerged from them: the French Antilles and French Guiana, the United States, Brazil, Haiti, the British Caribbean. They carry this memory into public debate, in Parliament, in museums and in families.

The Oriental slave trades left far fewer visible diasporas. Historians explain this by the castration of some of the men, the high proportion of women deported and the integration of the children of concubines, who were often born free. Tidiane N'Diaye contrasts about 1 million descendants of the Oriental slave trades with 70 million for the transatlantic slave trade, but neither figure is sourced.

2. States legislate on their own crimes

The French law of May 21, 2001, known as the Taubira law, recognizes as a crime against humanity the transatlantic slave trade, the Indian Ocean slave trade and slavery "perpetrated from the fifteenth century onward" against African, Amerindian, Malagasy and Indian populations. It names neither the trans-Saharan trades nor the intra-African trades. One plausible reading, which its defenders have not formulated in these terms, is that a Republic recognizing its crimes targets first those in which it took part.

Institutions followed: the Memorial to the Abolition of Slavery in Nantes (2012), the Mémorial ACTe in Guadeloupe (2015), the Foundation for the Memory of Slavery (2019). In April 2025, Emmanuel Macron acknowledged that the indemnity imposed on Haiti in 1825 had subjected it to "the unjust force of History." The United Kingdom, for its part, has opened its archives on the 20 million pounds paid to slave owners in 1833.

3. It is the best-documented slave trade

The transatlantic slave trade is the only one reconstructed voyage by voyage. The SlaveVoyages database records tens of thousands of expeditions and puts at 12.5 million the number of Africans embarked between 1501 and 1875. For the Oriental slave trades, estimates range from 8 to 25 million depending on the author, and the historian Klas Rönnbäck points out that for the centuries from 650 to 1400, the figures "can only be guessed." It is easier to talk about what can be measured, taught and shown.

4. A slavery based on color, with legacies still visible

In the Americas, slave status was hereditary and assigned by skin color, which was not the case in Rome, in Korea or in most of the Oriental slave trades, where the boundary was mainly religious. This racialization survived abolition: segregation in the United States until the 1960s, discrimination, wealth inequality. The slaveholding past thus remains tied to current political questions, which keeps it in the news.

5. A recent past, tied to debts and reparations

Brazil did not abolish slavery until 1888. Haiti paid off the 1825 indemnity and the related loans until 1921-1922. The abolitions compensated the masters, never the slaves. These facts fuel demands for reparations: CARICOM's ten-point plan (2014), the African Union's theme for 2025 devoted to reparations. A debate about money and state responsibility is bound to stay alive.

6. A tradition of self-criticism? A hypothesis

It is often argued that Western democracies, with their open archives, their freedom of the press and their abolitionist tradition, examine their own past more willingly than other societies. The argument is plausible, but no study establishes it: it must be presented as a hypothesis.

What the comparatists criticize

For Olivier Pétré-Grenouilleau, this focus produces a history paradoxically centered on Europe: it leaves in the shadows the Oriental and intra-African slave trades, and the role of African states such as Dahomey or the Sokoto Caliphate. He argues for a "global history" of the slave trades. Tidiane N'Diaye speaks of an Oriental slave trade that has been "concealed." Historians such as Pierre Nora, Pierre Vidal-Naquet and Guy Pervillé have also criticized a memory they consider selective and the fact that lawmakers pass judgment on history.

What the other historians reply

For Marcel Dorigny, Gérard Noiriel or Catherine Coquery-Vidrovitch, it is logical for a country to examine its own crimes first, and the Taubira law does not threaten research. They also point out that comparing slave trades has sometimes served to relativize: the narrative of the "Muslim" slave trade justified the colonization of Africa in the nineteenth century, and the argument of the other slave trades is frequently set against demands for reparations.

What this does not mean

That the transatlantic slave trade is talked about more does not mean that the other systems were milder, nor that they should be passed over in silence. Nor does it mean the opposite. This site describes each system with its own figures, rules and debates, so that everyone can compare on the evidence, without a scale of suffering.

Method and sources

How the figures are established

The volumes of slavery are rarely counts. They are reconstructions, more or less solid depending on the available archives.

The transatlantic slave trade is reconstructed voyage by voyage in the SlaveVoyages database (Eltis and Richardson), with imputations for the gaps. It is the best-measured slave trade.

The Oriental slave trades rest on travelers' accounts, partial registers and extrapolations (Austen, Lovejoy, Manning). Estimates vary threefold from one author to another.

The Tatar and Barbary raids are estimated by retrospective calculation from point-in-time numbers, a debated method.

Population shares (Rome, Korea, Sokoto, Siam) come from censuses or observers' estimates, and depend on the definition of slavery adopted.
